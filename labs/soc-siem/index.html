What Is a SOC and What Does a SOC Analyst Do?

A Security Operations Center (SOC) helps an organization monitor systems, assess security alerts, investigate potential threats, and coordinate an appropriate response.

From alert to evidence-based decision

SOC analysts review signals from computers, user accounts, applications, and network devices. They gather context, check whether an alert is supported by evidence, record what they found, and escalate or recommend a response according to the organization's procedures.

  • Monitor: review alerts and relevant security events.
  • Triage: assess urgency, scope, and available evidence.
  • Investigate: correlate related activity and look for alternative explanations.
  • Document: record facts, uncertainty, and recommended next steps.

Understanding SIEM and security logs

Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) systems bring together logs and events from multiple sources so analysts can search activity, correlate events, and create alerts. A SIEM alert is a lead to investigate—not automatic proof that an incident occurred.

  • Authentication logs: successful and failed sign-ins, account names, timestamps, and source details when available.
  • Windows event logs: operating-system and security events that help explain account and system activity.
  • Linux system and authentication logs: service events, sign-in activity, and system messages.
  • Network, firewall, and web logs: connection decisions, requests, source addresses, timestamps, and response codes.

Useful fields often include time, user or account, source address or device, action, outcome, and event source. Field names and availability vary by system.

Beginner SOC analyst practice: investigate a simulated alert

Scenario: A training alert reports 14 failed sign-in attempts followed by one successful sign-in from an unfamiliar device. This is fictional evidence for practice.

  1. Identify the account, timestamps, device or source details, and the outcome of each event.
  2. Check the sequence and time window. Determine whether the successful sign-in followed the failures closely.
  3. Look for related authentication events and available context, such as whether the device or location is expected.
  4. Write a short finding that separates observed facts from assumptions.
  5. Recommend a proportionate next step under an authorized response process. Do not declare a confirmed compromise based on this single alert alone.

Practice prompt: What evidence would you gather next, and what additional fact would help you decide whether the activity is suspicious? There may be more than one defensible answer when evidence is incomplete.

A safe alert-triage checklist

  1. Validate: confirm the alert's source, timestamp, and triggering condition.
  2. Scope: identify the account, host, service, and time range involved.
  3. Correlate: compare related records from relevant log sources.
  4. Assess: document impact, confidence, missing evidence, and plausible benign explanations.
  5. Escalate and document: follow authorized procedures and preserve the evidence needed for review.

Keep conclusions proportional to the evidence. Avoid making disruptive changes to real accounts or systems unless you have the appropriate authorization and follow the organization's process.

Build cybersecurity skills and portfolio evidence

A useful investigation record explains the question, the evidence reviewed, the observations, the conclusion and confidence level, and the next step. Never include real secrets, personal data, or confidential employer information in a public portfolio.

For a practice portfolio entry, record:

  • Scenario: the simulated alert and investigation question.
  • Evidence: relevant fictional log fields and the time sequence.
  • Assessment: what the evidence supports and what remains unknown.
  • Next step: a proportionate action or additional evidence to request.
